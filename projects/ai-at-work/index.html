01 / The challenge

Close the
judgment gap.

Organizations are rapidly adopting generative AI, but employees may lack clear guidance about appropriate, responsible, and effective workplace use. Knowing how to open an AI tool does not mean knowing what information to share, which outputs to trust, or when a human must lead.

The design challenge: help a beginner make a better decision at the point of use, with a small amount of learning that fits into a workday.

Audience

Capable professionals.
New AI decisions.

Employees beginning to incorporate generative AI into everyday work. The experience assumes no technical background and uses familiar tasks such as emails, briefings, and project updates.

Performance need

Use the right support.
Keep the right oversight.

Recognize useful assistance, protect sensitive input, verify important claims, and take responsibility for final work. These observable actions define the learning need.

Solution

A focused learning
experience with practice.

A 15–20 minute microlearning course combining short instruction, decisions, feedback, active practice, reflection, and a seven-question assessment.

Portfolio simulation. The audience and learning need are design assumptions for this project. No client discovery interviews, organizational pilot, or workplace impact study are claimed. In a client engagement, those assumptions would be validated with employees, managers, and privacy or security stakeholders.

02 / Learning objectives

Define the
workplace behavior.

The objectives move from understanding AI’s limits to applying judgment. Each connects to a practice activity and an assessment decision, so the experience measures more than recall.

01

Explain capabilities and limits

Explain what generative AI can and cannot reliably do in workplace settings.

02

Identify useful opportunities

Identify workplace tasks where generative AI can improve productivity.

03

Recognize workplace risks

Recognize privacy, confidentiality, bias, hallucination, and accuracy risks associated with AI.

04

Apply a decision framework

Apply a responsible decision-making framework before using or sharing AI-generated content.

03 / Learning strategy

Learn a little.
Make a decision.

The course alternates brief explanations with action. Learners choose, build, check, and reflect throughout the experience. Feedback explains the reasoning so an incorrect response becomes a chance to adjust judgment.

01 / Understand

What can AI do?

Build a simple mental model, then classify six tasks in “AI or Human?”

02 / Create

Write a useful brief

Construct a prompt with a goal, context, constraints, and output format.

03 / Decide

Navigate the risks

Explore five risks, three workplace scenarios, and the P.A.U.S.E. framework.

04 / Transfer

Put judgment to work

Complete five practice situations, a seven-question quiz, and a personal action.

Scenario-based learning

Confidential documents, unverifiable claims, and hiring decisions create a reason to apply the content.

Active practice

Prompt building makes the structure visible. Learners construct a useful input instead of simply reading a model.

Immediate feedback

Each practice choice explains what makes a response effective or risky. Scenario retries support correction.

Microlearning

Brief lessons and focused activities keep the learning task manageable within a typical workday.

Retrieval & decision practice

The final challenge and assessment revisit principles in different situations, requiring learners to make a choice.

Reflection

A specific next action connects the course to the learner’s own work and creates an intention to apply it.

06 / Alignment

Practice what
you measure.

The seven-question assessment uses applied multiple choice and select-all questions. Each question earns one point; select-all items require the complete correct set. Learners need 6 of 7 correct (86%) to meet the 80% threshold and can retry without limit.

07 / Evaluation strategy

Design for
evidence, not
assumptions.

A simplified Kirkpatrick approach separates the learner’s reaction and learning performance from later behavior and organizational results.

The course includes local checks; it has not been evaluated with an employee cohort. The proposed measures below describe what could be studied in an organizational implementation.

Included · Local learner feedback

Level 1 — Reaction

Ask about perceived usefulness at the end. Opening and closing confidence ratings provide additional self-report context alongside the learning check.

Measure: usefulness and confidence selections, kept in the learner’s browser. Confidence is also relevant to Level 2; it is not proof of competence.

Included · Local assessment

Level 2 — Learning

Use seven applied questions to check the objectives, show the score, explain responses, and allow targeted review and retries.

Measure: accuracy against the 80% pass threshold and topic-specific feedback. No aggregate learner results have been collected.

Proposed · Not measured

Level 3 — Behavior

After an approved pilot, use a short follow-up and manager-supported observation to see whether people check approved tools, protect information, verify claims, and review outputs.

Possible timing: 2–4 weeks after learning, with appropriate consent and privacy safeguards. This is a proposal, not an observed outcome.

Proposed · Not measured

Level 4 — Results

Explore task time alongside quality, inappropriate AI-use incidents, and adherence to organizational AI policies. Use a baseline and account for changes in tools, processes, and reporting.

Possible evidence: stakeholder-approved productivity and policy measures. No productivity improvement, risk reduction, or compliance result is claimed.

08 / Tools & delivery

Built for access.
Ready to explore.

The implementation supports the learning strategy through a lightweight browser experience. The portfolio demonstrates both instructional decisions and a working digital course.

Tools used

HTMLCSSJavaScriptGenerative AI-assisted development

The course works as a static website, including on GitHub Pages. It uses no database, external AI API, analytics service, or external font dependency. Answers, progress, and reflection stay in the learner’s browser.

Articulate Rise, Storyline, and an LMS were not used. The experience is not SCORM or xAPI packaged, and local progress does not sync between devices.

Accessibility by design

  • ✓Native buttons, form labels, and keyboard-accessible interactions
  • ✓Visible focus indicators, structured headings, and screen-reader announcements
  • ✓Text labels alongside progress and feedback colors
  • ✓Responsive layouts, reduced-motion support, and no timed activities

These are implemented design provisions, not a claim of formal WCAG certification. A real rollout would include testing with representative learners and assistive technology.
